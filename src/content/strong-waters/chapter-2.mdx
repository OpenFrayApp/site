Everything in the two catalogs is made by one of six methods, and a practitioner who knows
the six can read any receipt in the book without being told twice. This chapter is those
six, what each is good for, and what it costs in hours.

## Gathering

A component is a part of a thing, not the thing: the root, the cap, the inner bark, the
gum that runs when the bark is cut. Receipts name the part because the rest of the plant is
usually inert and occasionally worse.

Season and hour matter for the same reason. What a plant holds moves through it over a
year and over a day — into the root before winter, into the leaf in spring, out of the
flower by noon. A receipt that says _gathered before the dew lifts_ means it, and a
component gathered at the wrong hour is not weaker in some graded way; it is frequently
nothing at all.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  One will not gather what one cannot name. There is no receipt in this book that repairs the wrong
  plant, and there is one that will kill a man who mistook it, and I have written which.
</Voice>

## The six preparations

| Method       | Suits                             | Time      | Keeps               |
| ------------ | --------------------------------- | --------- | ------------------- |
| Infusion     | Leaf and flower                   | 1 hour    | 3 days              |
| Decoction    | Root, bark, and lichen            | 2–8 hours | 1 year, stoppered   |
| Tincture     | Anything the water will not take  | 30 days   | Years               |
| Unguent      | What must not be swallowed        | 4 hours   | 3 months            |
| Smoke        | What acts at once                 | Immediate | Indefinitely, dried |
| Distillation | Strength, from anything fermented | 8 hours   | Indefinitely        |

Each is worth knowing by what it suits and what it costs in hours:

- **Infusion** is steeping in water off the boil, the way one makes tea. It is the gentlest
  method and the one that loses the most; leaf and flower give up what they hold easily and
  would be spoiled by harder handling.
- **Decoction** is boiling and reducing, and it is the method most of chapter four uses.
  Roots, barks and lichens hold onto what they hold, and only sustained heat over hours
  will have it out of them. This is where the four-hour fire comes from, and where most of
  the failures come from too.
- **Tincture** is steeping in a strong water rather than in water. Some components will not
  give up anything to water at any temperature and give it up readily to spirit, which is
  the reason chapter three and chapter four are in the same book. A tincture is slow, keeps
  almost forever, and doses in drops rather than mouthfuls.
- **Unguent** is working a prepared component into rendered fat. It suits everything that
  must reach a body without being swallowed — the things that would gripe, and the things
  whose whole purpose is a small area of skin.
- **Smoke** is the shortest method and the shortest-acting. A component dried and burned
  reaches a body in a breath rather than a quarter of an hour, and leaves it as quickly.
  Nothing prepared this way lasts long enough to be measured in hours.
- **Distillation** is the still: heating something already fermented and catching what comes
  off it, which is stronger than what went in. It makes the back half of chapter three, and
  the base for every tincture in chapter four.

Water is the solvent under four of the six, and she has a view about it that a reader will
meet again.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  One will use soft water and one will use it fresh. What water is for is taking a thing out of
  another thing, and it does that whether one is holding a pot or not — which is why water alone
  rusts the bones, and why I will not have it drunk on its own in my house.
</Voice>

## Keeping, and knowing when it has turned

Every entry says how long its preparation keeps and how a practitioner knows it has gone
over — a smell that leaves, a color that arrives, a separation that will not stir back
together. These are not decorative. A turned preparation is rarely inert; it is usually
still active and no longer the dose on the label, which is the worst of both.

Stoppered glass, out of the light, cool. Everything else is detail.

## What magic actually does for this

She is direct about this, having been asked constantly.

<Voice title="Of the spells one is offered" by="Hesper Aldwine">
  Four spells are named to me by every apprentice who would rather cast than sit, and I will say
  plainly what each is worth at a bench.

Druidcraft will open a bud or a seed pod on the instant. That is a real thing and I use it, and
it will not fill a basket; one flower is one flower, and a decoction wants a handful.

Plant Growth enriches a countryside for a year and doubles what it bears. It is a farmer's spell
and a fine one. It will not bring tonight's root to tonight's pot.

Prestidigitation warms a cubic foot of dead stuff for an hour. A decoction wants four hours at an
even boil, so one will cast it thrice and still stand over a pot that never came up, for warming is
not boiling and never was.

Purify Food and Drink does exactly what it says, and what it says is ruinous here. See the fifth
chapter; I will not have it explained twice.

</Voice>

Two spells in the sixth chapter were written for this bench and do the work the four above
do not: Steady Fire, which holds a vessel at an even boil without fuel or attention, and
Hasten the Root, which brings one plant to a usable harvest rather than to a single
blossom. Neither is necessary. Both are the difference between an afternoon and a day.
