Drink is a preparation, though it is rarely called one. It is fermenting, and then
sometimes distilling, and the difference between a farmhouse and a still-house is a matter
of equipment rather than craft.

It is cataloged here for two reasons. It is the base of every tincture in the fourth
chapter, so a practitioner needs to know what is in the bottle before working with it. And
it is the substance her trade saw most of.

## What a measure is

Every drink below carries a **Strength**, which is how many measures one ordinary serving
counts as. A serving is a serving as the house pours it: a tankard of small beer and a
thimble of brandy are each one serving, and they are not each one measure.

A creature's **capacity** is 2 + its Constitution modifier, to a minimum of 1. Up to its
capacity in measures, a creature drinks without consequence.

Past that, every further measure calls a DC 10 Constitution saving throw. The Difficulty
Class rises by 2 for each saving throw the creature has already made since it began
drinking. On a failed save, the creature gains 1 level of Intoxication.

## The four levels

Intoxication has four levels, and the effects of a level include those below it:

- **Level 1, merry.** The creature has Advantage on saving throws against being Frightened,
  and Disadvantage on ability checks based on Wisdom.
- **Level 2, loose.** The creature has Disadvantage on ability checks based on Dexterity, Dexterity saving
  throws, and Wisdom saving throws.
- **Level 3, blind.** The creature has the Poisoned condition, and its Speed is reduced by
  10 feet.
- **Level 4, insensible.** The creature has the Unconscious condition for 1d4 hours. Damage
  does not end it. A creature that succeeds on a DC 15 Wisdom (Medicine) check rouses it
  for 1 minute, after which it drops again.

A creature has one level of Intoxication at a time, never several: a new failed save moves
it up the list rather than adding a second copy of where it already is.

Intoxication falls by one level for each hour a creature spends without drinking. Food and
water halve that hour. A creature that reached level 4 gains 1 Exhaustion level when its
Intoxication ends, and no amount of water prevents it.

## What drink asks for afterward

Drink carries a Craving, counted as [the first chapter](/strong-waters/chapter-1/) sets it
out. What adds to it is being drunk: a day on which a creature reached the third or fourth
level of Intoxication adds 1. A cup with a meal adds nothing, however many days in a row it
is taken.

A day counts as gone without only if the creature had no drink at all. One cup with supper
adds nothing to the count and takes nothing off it, so a creature that has one every evening
holds wherever it already stood for as long as it goes on doing it.

Everything in the tables below shares that one count, because they are all the same
substance. A body does not want ale and wine separately, and the Strength column decides
only how fast an evening arrives at the third level.

At 6 the count converts to an addiction, and it deepens by a degree at 12 and again at 18.
Drink is the only thing in this book whose three degrees are written out separately,
because it is the one a practitioner meets at every depth. A creature at the first degree
is not a smaller version of a creature at the third.

### The first degree

The creature wants one every day, and usually at the same hours: with the meal, after the
meal, before bed. It is not drunk, it does not get drunk, and nobody who lives with it
would call it anything but a drinker.

A day without is the first degree — Disadvantage on Wisdom and Charisma
checks — which in drink shows as a short temper, a slowness to settle, and poor
company. The creature will also go out of its way to get one: a walk it did not need to
take, a visit it did not want to make, an errand invented at the hour the inn is open.

Denied altogether, it sleeps badly. A creature at the first degree that goes to bed without
a drink still finishes a Long Rest and still regains its Hit Points, but removes no
Exhaustion level from that rest. Creatures at this degree commonly believe the drink is
what lets them sleep.

A creature can hold this degree for the whole of a life. The saving throws that end an
addiction are made only at the end of a day gone entirely without, and a creature that
takes one with supper every evening never has such a day. It never makes one, never gets
drunk enough to deepen, and stays where it is until something changes. It is the commonest
case in the book.

### The second degree

The creature drinks before the day begins, or before the thing in the day it is dreading,
and it drinks alone at least sometimes. It conceals how much, and is offended at being
asked.

A day without carries the first degree's temper and the second degree's Exhaustion level,
which in drink is the shake: hands that will not be still until the first one is down.
Money meant for other things goes on it.

### The third degree

The creature no longer drinks in order to be drunk. It drinks because it has begun, and
that is drink's excess: when it drinks at all, it drinks to the fourth level. It does not
intend to, it does not enjoy the last of it, and it cannot take one cup and stop.

What it does in the hours between the third level and the fourth is not the same in every
creature. Some become loud and vulgar, some become violent, most become tedious, and the
creature is not choosing which. A creature that reached the fourth level remembers nothing
of the hours after it reached the third, and learns what it did from whoever was there.

A creature that has been at this degree and has stopped is not afterward a creature that
can take one cup. The rule on that is general to this book and drink is its plainest case:
one glass, at a wedding, twenty years later, and the creature is at the third degree again
the same night, without passing through any count.

That is the whole of what this book counts about habitual drinking. What thirty years of it
does to a liver, a hand, a trade and a household is not a rule here. It happens too slowly
to count in days, and reliably enough that counting it would add nothing.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  I am asked where drinking ends and drunkenness begins, and I will not say, because the count does
  not care and neither do I. A man who takes one glass after every meal of his life and cannot take
  none is further along than a boy who was insensible at a wedding and has not touched it since. The
  question was never how much. It is whether one can leave it.
</Voice>

## Beer and ale

Malted grain, water, yeast, and — since the hop arrived — hops. The cheapest drink there
is, the safest thing to give a child, and the reason most towns have clean-ish water at
all.

### Small beer

What everyone drinks, including children and the sick, because the water is worse. Pale,
cloudy, and thin, with almost no head. It smells of wet bread. It tastes of wet bread, and
faintly sour at the end, and a person can drink it all day and work afterward.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  One will not distil small beer, nor tincture in it, nor keep it above four days. It is food, and
  one does not tincture in soup.
</Voice>

### Kvass

Stale rye bread fermented in water for a day or two, which is what a household does rather
than throw bread away. Brown and cloudy. It smells of sour bread and, faintly, of raisins.
It tastes tart and bready and barely of drink at all, and it is the cheapest thing in this
chapter that is not water.

### Mild

Young ale, sold within the week, brewed to be drunk in quantity by people who have worked.
Light brown, thin head. It smells faintly of malt and of the barrel. It tastes soft and a
little sweet, with almost no bitterness, and it is the drink of the working afternoon.

### Table ale

Darker than small beer and with something to it, brewed for the evening rather than the
working day. Brown to amber, a finger of head that goes quickly. It smells of bread and
malt; it tastes of malt first and then bitter from the hop, and the bitterness is what
tells a drinker how much they have had, if they are attending.

### Gruit ale

Ale bittered with herbs instead of hops — bog myrtle, yarrow, marsh rosemary — which is how
it was done before the hop and how it is still done where the hop will not grow. Amber,
hazy. It smells green and resinous over the malt. It tastes bitter in a different register
from a hopped ale, more like a tisane, and every gruit is its brewer's own receipt.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  A gruit is a metheglin's cousin and it deserves the same attention. Some of what goes in a gruit
  is in my fourth chapter, and a brewer who is generous with the myrtle has made something stronger
  than ale without meaning to.
</Voice>

### Porter

Dark ale brewed from browned malt and aged in vats until it has lost its rough edge. Deep
brown, red at the edge against a light. It smells of toast and of the cellar. It tastes
dry, roasted, and long, and it travels better than anything else on this list.

### Stout

Black, or near enough that a candle behind the glass shows red at the edge and nothing
else. A thick head that stays. It smells roasted, almost burnt, and of coffee to those who
have met coffee. It tastes of the same, and sweeter than the smell promises, and it is
heavy enough that most drinkers stop before the arithmetic catches them.

### Sour ale

Ale left open to whatever is in the air of one particular brewhouse, then aged for a year
or three. Gold to brown, still or lively. It smells of vinegar, hay and apples. It tastes
sharply sour and then complicated, and it is either the best thing in this chapter or
undrinkable, according to who is asked.

### Braggot

Ale and honey fermented together, which is older than either being made separately and is
the drink of places too far north for grapes. Deep amber, thick, with a head that holds. It
smells of malt and honey at once. It tastes of malt first and finishes sweet, and it is
heavier than it drinks.

### Barley wine

Ale brewed as strong as ale can be brewed and then kept for years, until it stops behaving
like beer. Deep russet, almost still. It smells of dried fruit, of toffee, of sherry. It
tastes sweet, thick and hot, and it is served in a cup rather than a tankard for reasons
that become obvious.

## Cider, perry and mead

Fruit and honey, fermented — the drink of orchards and of people who keep bees, and the
oldest of anything in this chapter.

| --------- | -------- | ------- | ----- |
| Cider     | 1        | Tankard | 3 cp  |
| Hydromel  | 1        | Cup     | 4 cp  |
| Perry     | 1        | Tankard | 4 cp  |
| Mead      | 2        | Cup     | 8 cp  |
| Cyser     | 2        | Cup     | 1 sp  |
| Melomel   | 2        | Cup     | 1 sp  |
| Pyment    | 2        | Cup     | 1 sp  |
| Metheglin | 2        | Cup     | 2 sp  |

### Cider

Pressed apple, fermented. Straw to deep gold, sometimes still and sometimes lively. It
smells of apples that have gone a little further than eating apples. It tastes sharp, then
sweet, then sharp again, and the sweetness is what makes it dangerous — it goes down like
something weaker than it is.

### Hydromel

Mead made thin, with far more water than honey, and drunk young because it will not keep.
Pale gold, almost watery. It smells faintly of honey. It tastes light and barely sweet, and
it is what a household makes when the hive was small.

### Perry

The same made from pears, and the better drink, though it is rarer because the trees take
a lifetime and the fruit will not keep. Paler than cider, almost colorless. It smells
faintly of pears and mostly of nothing. It tastes soft, with none of cider's sharpness,
which is precisely why people who are careful with cider are careless with perry.

### Mead

Honey and water, fermented, which is the oldest of them and the one every people claims to
have invented. Gold to amber, thick in the glass. It smells of honey and of the flowers the
bees had. It tastes sweet and then hot, and the heat arrives later than a drinker expects.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  One will keep mead a year before drinking it and five before judging it. What is drunk at a month
  is not bad mead; it is not yet mead.
</Voice>

### Cyser

Apples and honey fermented together — cider's rich cousin, and the drink an orchard makes
when it has bees as well. Deep gold. It smells of apples and honey with something like
sherry behind them. It tastes sweet and then dry, and it keeps for years and improves for
most of them.

### Melomel

Mead with fruit other than apples or grapes put in at the fermenting — cherry, raspberry,
elderberry, whatever the hedge gave that year. Colored by the fruit, from pink to near
black. It smells of the fruit first. It tastes of honey underneath and the fruit on top,
and it is the mead most likely to be mistaken for wine by somebody being polite.

### Pyment

Honey and grapes fermented together, which is what a vineyard with bees makes and what
everybody else calls showing off. Rose to deep gold. It smells of honey and vine flowers.
It tastes rich and dry at once.

### Metheglin

Mead with the herbs put in — the maker's own receipt, and every maker's is secret and most
are the same four herbs. Colored by whatever went in, from gold to a startling green. It
smells of honey underneath and spice on top. It tastes of the spice first and the honey
after, and a good one is the finest drink in this chapter.

Metheglin is where this chapter and the next touch. Some of what is put in is a kitchen
herb. Some of it is not, and an entry in the fourth chapter that says _taken in drink_ is
often taken exactly this way.

### Sack mead

Mead made with so much honey that the yeast gives up before it has eaten it all, leaving a
drink that is both very strong and frankly sweet. Deep amber, syrupy in the glass. It
smells of honey, beeswax and dried fruit. It tastes like a dessert and drinks like a
spirit, which is the trap.

## Wine

Grapes, fermented, and everything a cellar does to them afterward. The range in this
section is the widest in the book: the same fruit makes both the thinnest drink in the
chapter and the most expensive.

### Country wine

Anything but grapes, fermented by somebody's grandmother: elderflower, birch sap,
dandelion, nettle, cowslip. Colored by whatever it was, from water-pale to a startling
yellow. It smells of the flower or the sap and of the cellar it sat in. It tastes thin,
sweet, and faintly of the year it was made, and every house swears by its own and
disparages the next village's.

### Table wine

Fermented grape, thin and sour and universal. Red or straw, usually cut with water at
table. It smells of vinegar at the edges when it is old, which is most of the time. It
tastes sharp and thin, and its virtue is that there is a great deal of it.

### Resined wine

Wine sealed with pine resin, which keeps it from turning and gets into it permanently.
Pale gold. It smells of pine and turpentine. It tastes of the same, and of wine somewhere
underneath, and people raised on it prefer it to anything in this section.

### Claret

Light red wine drunk young, shipped in quantity, the ordinary good wine of anywhere with
money. Clear ruby. It smells of red fruit and a little of the barrel. It tastes bright and
dry and finishes short, and it is meant to be drunk rather than discussed.

### Sack

Fortified wine — wine with spirit added, so that it keeps at sea and travels. Deep amber.
It smells of raisins and of wood. It tastes sweet, nutty, and hot, and it is the drink
most often underestimated by people who think of it as wine.

### Canary

Sack's sweeter cousin from warmer islands, shipped in butts and much admired. Deep gold.
It smells of honey, apricot and wood. It tastes sweet and hot and rounder than sack, and
it is the wine most often named in the letters of people complaining about the price.

### Malmsey

The sweetest and heaviest of the fortified wines, dark as treacle and nearly as thick. It
smells of raisins, burnt sugar and old wood. It tastes intensely sweet and then dry at the
very end, which is what saves it, and a cup of it is a considerable undertaking.

### Straw wine

Grapes dried on straw mats until they are half raisins, then pressed and fermented, which
takes a small crop and makes a smaller one. Deep gold to amber. It smells of apricot,
honey and nuts. It tastes concentrated in every direction at once.

### Fine wine

The same fruit as the table wine and nothing else the same: a named vineyard, a good year,
and ten or fifteen years in a cellar that somebody paid to keep cool. Deep and clear, and
it throws a sediment that a careful house decants off. It smells of the fruit it was and
then of a dozen things that were never in it. It tastes long — the flavor arrives, changes
twice, and is still there a minute after the mouthful is gone, which is the whole of what
the money buys.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  One will not tincture in fine wine, nor cook with it, nor give it to a patient. It is ten gold the
  bottle and it does nothing that two silver of common will not do as well, and I have said so to
  the faces of men who did not care for hearing it.
</Voice>

## Spirits

Everything above, put through a still. A spirit is not a different drink so much as the
same drink with the water taken out, and everything in this section is dangerous in a way
nothing in the previous three is: a thimble looks like nothing and is four measures.

### Aqua vitae

The first of the distilled, and the plainest: what comes off a still charged with wine.
Colorless, oily in the glass. It smells of heat and faintly of the wine it was. It tastes
of burning, and then of nothing, and then of the wine again a moment later.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  Aqua vitae is the base of near every tincture in the next chapter, and one will want it clean
  rather than good. What is pleasant to drink carries too much of its own flavor into a preparation,
  and a tincture is not improved by tasting of grapes.
</Voice>

### Marc

Distilled from what the wine press left behind — skins, stems and pips, which still hold
enough to be worth a still. Colorless to faint straw, harsh. It smells green and stalky.
It tastes rough and hot and unmistakably of the vineyard floor, and it is what the workers
drink while the good wine goes elsewhere.

### Rum

Distilled from cane sugar or its molasses, which means from somewhere warm, which means it
arrived by ship. Pale straw to dark brown according to the wood it sat in. It smells sweet
and slightly burnt, of treacle and hot iron. It tastes sweet, then hot, then of the
molasses again, and the cheap sort is called kill-devil by the people who drink it and are
honest about it.

### Bread spirit

Distilled from rye or wheat mash, filtered through charcoal until it smells of nearly
nothing, and drunk cold. Colorless. It smells faintly of bread and of cold metal. It tastes
of heat and almost nothing else, which is the entire intention.

### Arrack

Distilled from palm sap or from rice, made east of anywhere this book was written, and
arriving in small casks at large prices. Colorless to faint gold. It smells sharp, floral,
and faintly of bread. It tastes hot and then unexpectedly sweet, and it is the base of the
best punch in this chapter.

### Brandy

Aqua vitae kept in wood until it stops being aqua vitae — the same spirit, the same grape,
and years of patience between them. Amber to deep russet. It smells of fruit, of wood, and
of something like flowers. It tastes hot and round and finishes long, and it is the drink
this book recommends to anybody who wants to understand what wood does to a spirit.

### Geneva

Distilled grain flavored with juniper, made everywhere, made badly nearly everywhere.
Colorless. It smells of juniper and of pine resin. It tastes of juniper and bitterness, and
a bad one tastes of turpentine, and there is a great deal of bad one.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  Juniper is in it because juniper covers what the still did not take out. One will remember this
  when buying cheap geneva, and one will remember what it is covering.
</Voice>

### Fruit spirit

Cherries, plums, or pears fermented whole and then distilled — a great weight of fruit for
a small bottle, which is the price. Colorless, however dark the fruit was. It smells
intensely of the fruit and of its stone. It tastes hot, dry, and of the fruit's kernel
rather than its flesh, which surprises people expecting something sweet.

### Usquebaugh

Distilled from malted grain and kept in wood for years, which is what gives it the color it
did not start with. Amber to deep brown. It smells of smoke, of the wood, and of the malt
beneath both. It tastes hot, then sweet, then dry, and it is the drink in this chapter that
most rewards drinking slowly and most punishes not.

### Wormwood water

Spirit redistilled over wormwood, anise and fennel, sold green and drunk cut with water,
which turns it cloudy. It smells of anise and of bitter herbs. It tastes of anise first and
wormwood a long time afterward.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  Wormwood is in my fourth chapter as well, and one will read that entry before drinking much of
  this. A little wormwood settles a stomach. A great deal of wormwood is not drink at all, and the
  people who sell this by the bottle would rather one did not ask how much is in it.
</Voice>

## Liquors

A liquor is a spirit that has been made to taste of something else and then sweetened: a
herb, a nut, a peel, a stone, a bean, steeped in aqua vitae until the spirit has taken what
it holds, then let down with sugar syrup. It is the fourth chapter's tincture made for
pleasure rather than for dose, and by the same method.

The sugar and the water bring the Strength down, so a liquor is weaker than the spirit it
was built on and stronger than a wine. It is served in a small glass and drunk slowly, and
the sweetness hides the spirit as reliably as it does in a mixture.

### Aniseed water

Anise and fennel steeped in a clean spirit and sweetened, sold clear and drunk either neat
or let down with water, which turns it milky. It smells of aniseed and faintly of liquorice.
It tastes sweet first and cold at the back of the throat afterward.

A glass taken after a heavy meal settles a stomach, in the same small way ginger tea does.

### Black bitters

Gentian root, bitter peel and a dozen herbs the maker will not list, steeped long and
sweetened barely enough to be drinkable. Near black, syrupy against the glass. It smells
medicinal. It tastes bitter, then sweet, then bitter again for some while after, and it is
drunk at the end of an evening by people who insist it is good for them.

### Honey cordial

Usquebaugh sweetened with heather honey and spiced lightly, which is what a still-house
does with the barrels that came out rougher than intended. Deep gold, thick, clinging. It
smells of honey over smoke. It tastes sweet and then hot, and the smoke arrives last.

### Cherry cordial

Sour cherries crushed with their stones and steeped in spirit for a season, which takes a
faint almond note from the kernels. Deep red, clear. It smells of cherry and marzipan. It
tastes sweet, sharp and faintly of almond.

### Orange cordial

The dried peel of bitter oranges steeped in spirit and sweetened, made anywhere the fruit
can be shipped dry. Pale gold to orange. It smells of peel and of nothing else. It tastes
sweet and bitter at once, and it is the liquor most used in a kitchen rather than a glass.

### Stonefruit milk

Apricot kernels ground and steeped, sweetened heavily and let down with cream, which is why
it will not keep above a week. Ivory, opaque. It smells of almonds and cream. It tastes of
marzipan, and it is the first drink most people are given that they enjoy, which is worth
a practitioner knowing.

### Coffee cordial

Roasted bean steeped in bread spirit and sweetened hard, drunk cold. Near black. It smells
of coffee and burnt sugar. It tastes of both, and of the spirit last.

It carries no waking at all. Whatever the roasting put into the coffee does not survive the
steeping, so a creature that drinks it for the purpose is drinking sugar and spirit.

### Cloister green

A closed order's own list of herbs, distilled and steeped and sweetened over a year, sold
in a sealed flask with the order's mark on it and imitated everywhere. Green going gold with
age. It smells of a hillside. It tastes of a great many things in sequence, and no two
makers' agree.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  I have taken a green liquor apart twice and got twenty-nine herbs out of it the first time and
  thirty-one the second, and I could not swear the flask was the same recipe. What one is buying is
  a year of somebody's patience, and I say that as a compliment, which is not how it is usually
  taken.
</Voice>

## Sparkles

Her word for a drink that is lively in the glass and carries nothing to be drunk. Some
springs come up sparkling on their own and are bottled at the source; the rest are made,
by setting a sweetened infusion to work briefly and stopping it before it becomes a drink,
or by charging water from a still-house's own apparatus.

Nothing in this section counts toward capacity or Intoxication, adds to a Craving, or does
anything a body must recover from. They are here because a house that sells drink sells
these beside it, because children, abstainers and anybody working in the morning drink
them, and because half the mixtures in the next section are built on them.

Three of them do something worth writing down, and the rest are pleasant:

- **Ginger sparkle** carries ginger tea's virtue in a colder form. For 1 hour after a
  tankard, a creature has Advantage on saving throws against the Poisoned condition caused
  by the motion of a ship or a coach, or by food that has turned.
- **Bitter sparkle** settles a stomach after a heavy meal, in the same small way aniseed
  water does.
- **Spring sparkle** replaces what a day's sweating takes, so a creature that drinks a
  tankard after a forced march or a shift at a forge recovers as though it had eaten and
  drunk properly, for the purpose of going without food or water for that day.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  A sparkle is water with something in it and bubbles besides, and I will not be drawn further than
  that. It is not medicine. It is the only thing I sell to a man's children and to the man himself
  on the same morning, and it has kept more people out of the ale before noon than any argument I
  have made.
</Voice>

## Mixtures

Everything above is one thing fermented, and sometimes distilled. Everything below is two
or more of them put together with heat, spice, eggs, cream, fruit or sugar — which is a
preparation as much as anything in the fourth chapter is, and is made in the same kitchen
by the same person on a colder evening.

A mixture's Strength is what went into it, not what it tastes of, and that is the whole
danger of the section. Sugar, cream and spice hide spirit completely; a punch made of
arrack tastes of lemon and drinks like four measures, because it is four measures. Where a
mixture is cut with water, milk or cream it carries less than its parts, and the entry says
so; where it is heated with a spirit it carries all of them.

<Voice title="Of ice, and of short measure" by="Hesper Aldwine">
  One will ask for a mixture without ice, and one will watch the face of whoever is asked.

A house that ices a punch is not cooling it for one's comfort. Ice takes up the room the
spirit was meant to occupy and then becomes water in the cup, so that one pays for four
measures, receives two, and drinks the difference back as it melts. The bowl looks full
throughout, which is the entire trick.

Taken without ice a mixture is warmer and honest, and one may count what one has had. And
a cup that has been ice is water, and water alone rusts the bones, which I have written
elsewhere and will write again.

</Voice>

A mixture carries a rule of its own only where it was made for something other than
pleasure. Caudle is invalid food, purl is the morning after, aqua mirabilis is sold as a
remedy, and those three do something. The rest were made to be enjoyed, and an apothecary
has nothing to add about them beyond what is in the Strength column.

### Caudle

Ale thickened with egg and oatmeal, spiced, and drunk hot — invalid food more than drink,
given to the sick, the newly delivered, and anybody who has been out in weather. Pale
brown, thick enough to coat a spoon. It smells of oats and nutmeg. It tastes bland and
comforting, and it is the only thing in this chapter a physician will hand you without
comment.

A creature that drinks a cup of caudle and then rests has that rest count as though it
were taken warm and fed, which is the whole of what the sick want from it.

### Purl

Ale warmed with wormwood and ginger, bitter as a rebuke, drunk in the morning by people who
were drinking something else the night before. Brown and cloudy. It smells of bitter herbs
over malt. It tastes appalling and works.

A creature that drinks a tankard of purl reduces its Intoxication by 1 level, and can
benefit from purl only once between Long Rests.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  Purl is the only receipt in this book people thank me for and then go on doing the thing that made
  them want it. I have stopped saying anything and I have raised the price.
</Voice>

### Posset

Hot milk curdled deliberately with ale or with sack, sweetened and spiced, drunk before
bed. Ivory, separated into a thick top and a thin under-layer that is the good part. It
smells of warm milk and nutmeg. It tastes rich and faintly sour where the curdling took,
and it is the drink most associated with being cared for.

A **sack posset** is the same made with sack instead of ale, which is a different drink at
a different price and carries two measures rather than one. It is what a house serves at a
wedding, and it is the reason weddings go the way they do.

### Negus

Sack, hot water, sugar, lemon and grated nutmeg — a supper drink and a children's-party
drink both, depending on how much sack goes in and who is watching. Pale amber, steaming.
It smells of nutmeg and hot lemon. It tastes mild, warm, and much weaker than it is.

### Syllabub

Cream whipped with wine or cider and sugar until it holds — half a drink and half a
pudding, served in a glass so the separated layers show. White above, amber below. It
smells of cream and of whatever went in. It tastes sweet and cold and barely of drink at
all, which is why people have four.

### Flip

Ale, spirit, egg and sugar, mixed and then heated by plunging a red-hot iron into the
tankard, which makes it froth and gives it a scorched note nothing else has. Deep brown
under a tall head. It smells burnt and sweet. It tastes of caramel and singed cream, and
the iron is not optional — a flip mixed cold is merely a bad drink.

The spirit in a flip is poured by the gill and not by the thimble, which is why it is the
strongest thing in this chapter and why nobody believes that until afterward.

### Shrub

Fruit steeped in vinegar and sugar until it gives up everything it has, then cut with
spirit and kept. Ruby to dark amber, syrupy. It smells sharply of the fruit and then of
the vinegar behind it. It tastes sour, then sweet, then hot, and it keeps a summer's fruit
until midwinter, which is the point of it.

### Hippocras

Nothing in it carries drink but the wine, so it is a wine's Strength however hot and sweet
it tastes — which she notes is the opposite of the trap the rest of this section sets.

Wine steeped with honey, cinnamon, ginger and grains of paradise, then strained through a
long conical bag of felt that the trade calls a sleeve. Deep red-brown, and perfectly
clear if the sleeve was doing its work. It smells of the spice before the wine. It tastes
sweet and hot and long.

<Voice title="Of the straining" by="Hesper Aldwine">
  One will hang the sleeve and let it drip, and one will not squeeze it, however late the hour and
  however many are waiting. A squeezed hippocras is cloudy and bitter both, and the man who squeezed
  it always says the sleeve was at fault.
</Voice>

### Punch

Spirit, citrus, sugar, water and spice — five things, which is what the name means, and
the reason every house makes it differently is that only the proportions are secret. Color
according to the spirit and the fruit, from pale gold to deep red. It smells of lemon and
whatever it was built on. It tastes cold, sweet and sharp, and it hides its spirit more
completely than anything else in this chapter.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  Punch is made in a bowl and drunk in cups, and nobody counts cups. I have been called to more
  houses for punch than for every simple in the fourth chapter together, and the people who sent for
  me were always surprised.
</Voice>

### Aqua mirabilis

The apothecary's own drink: a clean spirit redistilled over herbs — sage, marjoram, mace,
cloves, lemon peel — until it comes off sweet and green-smelling. Faintly gold, oily.
It smells of a garden in the sun. It tastes hot, herbal and very slightly sweet, and it
costs what it costs because a still is expensive and it is charged twice.

It is a spirit charged twice and cut with nothing, so a thimble carries as much as a thimble
of anything in the spirits table. A creature that drinks one has Advantage on the next
saving throw it makes against cold weather or exposure within the hour.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  It is sold as a cure for everything from palsy to grief, and I have made my share of that money.
  What it is, is a warm drink for a cold road, and I will say so to anybody who asks me plainly.
</Voice>

### Sparkle punch

A bowl of mixed sparkles with cut fruit and sugar, made for the same table as a punch and
set beside it. Coloured by whatever went in. It smells of lemon and berries. It tastes
sweet, sharp and cold.

It carries nothing at all. A creature can drink the bowl and walk home.

### Shandy

Ale cut half and half with ginger sparkle, drunk at midday by people who mean to go back to
work afterward. Pale and lively, with a head that goes at once. It smells of malt and
ginger. It tastes lighter and sharper than the ale it was made from.

Cut with a sparkle, it carries half what the ale carried.

### Spritz

Wine let down with a sparkle and a twist of peel, served cold and slowly in the afternoon.
The colour of the wine and paler. It smells of the wine and the peel together. It tastes
light, dry and faintly bitter at the end.

The sparkle is water, so a spritz carries less than the wine that went into it — one
measure from a cup of table wine rather than two.

### Cordial long

A glass of liquor filled up with a sparkle, which is what a house pours for somebody who
wants the flavour of a thing without the evening that usually follows it. Coloured by the
liquor. It smells and tastes of whichever one went in, thinned and lengthened.

It carries what the liquor carried, spread over a longer drink.

### Long spirit

A thimble of spirit in a tankard of sparkle, with citrus if the house has any. Near
colourless, lively, and served cold enough that the spirit is hard to find in it. It smells
faintly of the citrus. It tastes of almost nothing.

A long spirit is four measures. The sparkle adds no drink and takes none away, so it
carries exactly what the thimble carried, in a tankard that drinks like a sparkle. It is
the plainest case of what this section warns about, and it is ordered by people who would
not order four thimbles.

## Without drink

Not everything a house puts in a cup is fermented, and some of what is not does more than
water does. Water itself is not in this table and will not be, and her reason is given
below and is the only opinion in this book she asks anybody to take on faith. Nothing here counts toward capacity or Intoxication. These are the mild end of
what the fourth chapter catalogs at strength, and a practitioner who understands why a cup
of coffee works understands why emberfoot works, and how much further it goes.

| ---------- | ------- | ----- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Broth      | Bowl    | 2 cp  | Counts as a day's food                           |
| Ginger tea | Cup     | 2 cp  | Advantage against sickness of motion or bad food |
| Chamomile  | Cup     | 2 cp  | Eases the way into sleep                         |
| Switchel   | Tankard | 3 cp  | Advantage against heat and exertion              |
| Willow tea | Cup     | 4 cp  | Dulls pain, and nothing else                     |
| Tea        | Cup     | 6 cp  | Advantage against sleep for 1 hour               |
| Coffee     | Cup     | 8 cp  | Advantage against sleep for 2 hours              |
| Chocolate  | Cup     | 3 sp  | Advantage against sleep for 2 hours, and food    |

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  Water alone rusts the bones. I have said so to physicians, to my own apprentices, and to a man
  from a college who came all the way out here to tell me otherwise, and I say it still. Put
  something in it — a leaf, a grain, a spoonful of anything on this page — and it is a drink. On its
  own it is a solvent, and one is made largely of what it dissolves.
</Voice>

### Broth

Bones and trimmings simmered a day and skimmed. Cloudy gold, with fat at the top that a
careful cook takes off and a hungry one does not. It smells of meat and of the kitchen.
It tastes of salt and of very little else, and it is the first thing given to anybody who
has been ill.

A bowl of broth counts as a day's rations for the purpose of going without food.

### Ginger tea

Ginger root, bruised and steeped. Pale yellow, cloudy. It smells hot and green. It tastes
sharp and warming at the back of the throat.

For 1 hour after drinking it, a creature has Advantage on saving throws against the
Poisoned condition caused by the motion of a ship or a coach, or by food that has turned.

### Chamomile

The dried flower, steeped. Pale gold. It smells of apples and of hay. It tastes faintly
bitter and faintly sweet, and it is given to children and to people who cannot settle.

A creature that drinks a cup before resting is not woken by ordinary noise, and wakes on
its own at the hour it intended.

### Switchel

Water, vinegar, ginger and molasses, drunk cold in quantity by people bringing in a
harvest. Cloudy brown. It smells sour and gingery. It tastes tart and salty-sweet and is
far better than it sounds when one has been in a field since dawn.

For 1 hour after drinking a tankard, a creature has Advantage on Constitution saving throws
made against extreme heat or against exhaustion from a forced march.

### Willow tea

Willow bark, stripped in spring and steeped bitter. Brown, astringent. It smells of wet
bark. It tastes bitter enough that most people take it with honey.

It dulls an ache for an hour or two. It removes no condition, restores no Hit Points, and
does nothing whatever about the cause, and she is emphatic that people mistake the second
for the first.

### Tea

Dried leaves from a great distance away, steeped brief and drunk hot. Amber. It smells
grassy and faintly smoky. It tastes bitter and clean, and it costs what it costs because of
where it came from.

For 1 hour after drinking a cup, a creature has Advantage on saving throws made to stay
awake.

### Coffee

A roasted bean, ground and boiled, drunk black and bitter by people who have work to
finish. Near black, with a pale crown when fresh. It smells burnt and rich. It tastes
bitter, and then bitter, and people persist anyway.

For 2 hours after drinking a cup, a creature has Advantage on saving throws made to stay
awake.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  Coffee is the gentlest thing in my book that does anything at all, and it is where I send people
  who ask me for emberfoot and do not need it. The one is a cup and two hours. The other is four
  hours and a debt, and the debt is in the fourth chapter under its own name.
</Voice>

### Chocolate

The ground bean beaten into hot milk or water with sugar and a little chili, which is how
it is drunk where it comes from and how it should be drunk everywhere. Dark brown, thick,
frothed. It smells rich and faintly bitter. It tastes of all three of its parts at once,
and it is a meal as much as a drink.

For 2 hours after drinking a cup, a creature has Advantage on saving throws made to stay
awake. A cup also counts as half a day's rations.

## Where it is poured

A house stocks what its custom can pay for, and the standards of living the rules already
name are as good a measure of that as any. The table below is what a house of each sort has
behind it. Each level includes everything above it: a comfortable inn pours the poor house's
ale as well as its own claret, usually at the poor house's price.

| House            | What it pours                                                                                                                 |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Wretched**     | Whatever has been left. Small beer if the water is bad, and often not that                                                    |
| **Squalid**      | Small beer, kvass, mild, spring sparkle, and one unnamed spirit poured from an unmarked jar                                   |
| **Poor**         | Table ale, cider, country wine, purl, caudle, birch and ginger sparkle, marc, bread spirit                                    |
| **Modest**       | Porter, stout, table wine, mead, hydromel, geneva, rum, aniseed water, shandy, negus, posset                                  |
| **Comfortable**  | Gruit, sour ale, braggot, perry, claret, resined wine, brandy, usquebaugh, most liquors, hippocras, spritz, shrub             |
| **Wealthy**      | Barley wine, sack, canary, cyser, melomel, metheglin, arrack, fruit spirit, aqua mirabilis, punch, long spirit, cream sparkle |
| **Aristocratic** | Fine wine, malmsey, straw wine, sack mead, pyment, cloister green, and a cellar rather than a shelf                           |

Two things do not follow the table. Wormwood water is sold wherever it is not forbidden and
nowhere it is, so it turns up in a squalid back room and an aristocratic study and rarely in
between. And a house of any level will have one bottle of something far above its station,
kept for a particular customer or a particular occasion, which is where most of the
interesting evenings in her book begin.
