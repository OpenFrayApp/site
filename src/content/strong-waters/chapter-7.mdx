Everything in the two catalogs has a cost, and the cost arrives after the effect does. This
chapter is what a practitioner does about it.

There are four ways the substances in this book cause harm. They are not versions of one
another and their treatments do not substitute for one another, so they are separated
first. Then the ladder of care, which is the order in which things are tried. Then each of
the four in turn, and last what the spells in the Basic Rules reach.

## Four things, and telling them apart

What arrives at a shop door is one of four things. They are told apart by two questions:
whether the harm ends on its own, and how long there is to decide.

- **The morning.** A creature has drunk past its capacity and is coming down through the
  four levels of Intoxication. It ends on its own at one level an hour whatever anybody
  does, so the work is comfort and nothing else.
- **The comedown.** A simple has ended and what it was holding off has arrived — the sleep
  it deferred, the pain it covered, or both. This also ends on its own, but over days
  rather than hours, and it is the one most often mistaken for illness.
- **The wanting.** A body has begun to expect a substance, which the first chapter counts
  as Craving. It does not end on its own, and left alone it converts to an addiction, which
  then deepens by degrees until the creature is arranging its life around the substance.
- **The overdose.** More of a substance in one sitting than the entry's threshold allows,
  still acting. It is the only one of the four that is an emergency, it is measured in
  minutes rather than hours, and it is the only one where the wrong first move can kill.

The first question in every case is the third of the three things she asks in
[the first chapter](/strong-waters/chapter-1/): know what the person in front of you has
already taken, how much, and when. All four arrive as a creature that is not well, and
three of the four look much alike at the door. Without the answer a practitioner is
guessing which of them this is, and the treatments do not overlap enough for a guess to be
harmless — the emetic that saves an overdose does nothing whatever for a comedown, and the
days of attendance that answer a craving are time an overdose does not have.

## The ladder of care

Care is a ladder. The rungs are ordered by what they cost — in coin, in preparation, and in
somebody's time — rather than by what they achieve, and a practitioner climbs from the
bottom and stops at the first rung that answers the case. Most cases stop on the second.

1. **Water, warmth and time.** A blanket, a quiet corner, and somebody looking in every
   hour. It is the whole treatment for the morning and most of the treatment for a
   comedown, both of which end on their own and need only to be survived comfortably.
2. **The kitchen.** Purl for a bad morning, caudle for somebody who has been cold, broth
   for somebody who has not eaten, willow tea for an ache, chamomile for the night after.
   The first two are in [the fourth chapter](/strong-waters/chapter-4/) and the rest in
   [the third](/strong-waters/chapter-3/), all of them cost coppers, and between them they
   answer more of what comes through a door than every rung above them together.
3. **The bench.** Preparations made for the purpose rather than for pleasure: the emetic,
   the charcoal, and a strong infusion of whatever the entry names. These need an
   apothecary rather than a kitchen, and they are the rungs that decide an overdose.
4. **The hand.** Keeping a body warm, turned, and breathing. It needs no preparation, no
   spell and no coin, and it needs one person doing nothing else for as long as it goes on.
   It treats nothing. It keeps a creature alive long enough for anything else on the ladder
   to be tried, which in an overdose is often the only thing that matters.
5. **Magic.** What the spells reach, set out at the end of this chapter, and the remedies
   in the eighth chapter, which were written for the cases the Basic Rules leave alone.

One term is used throughout the rest of this chapter and is defined here. A day of
**attendance** is a day a creature spends fed, watered, warm and rested, out of reach of
the substance, with somebody sober keeping the count. It is the second rung applied for a
whole day rather than for an evening. It appears three times below — against a comedown,
against Craving, and against an addiction's saving throws — and it means the same thing in
each.

An overdose in progress is the one case that does not start at the bottom. It starts at the
third rung or the fourth, according to whether the dose has taken hold yet, and the first
two rungs are what happens afterward, once the emergency is over.

## The morning

Intoxication comes down on its own at one level an hour, and food and water halve that
hour; both rules are in [the fourth chapter](/strong-waters/chapter-4/). Nothing on the
ladder shortens that clock by more than a single level, so the work is making the hours
tolerable rather than removing them, and what it takes is cheap.

What is worth having on hand:

- A tankard of purl reduces Intoxication by 1 level, once between Long Rests. It is the
  only preparation in this book that takes a level off directly, which is why the fourth
  chapter gives it a rule of its own rather than a line in a table.
- Broth, bread and water carry a creature through the remaining hours faster than a bed
  alone, because food and water halve each hour, and because a creature that is being fed
  is being looked at every hour as well.
- A creature that reached the fourth level gains 1 Exhaustion level when its Intoxication
  ends. That level is removed by a Long Rest like any other, and nothing below the fifth
  rung removes it faster, so a creature that drank itself insensible loses the day after.

The morning is also where drink's own count is kept. A day on which a creature reached the
third or fourth level of Intoxication adds 1 to its Craving for drink. A practitioner who
treats the same creature's morning repeatedly is watching that count climb.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  I am asked for a cure for the morning perhaps twice a week, and I sell purl, and I say what purl
  is: a bitter drink that takes one level off and no more. There is no cure for the morning. The
  morning is the account being read back, and I have never known a body to be argued out of its
  arithmetic.
</Voice>

## The comedown

A simple of the waking gives a body nothing it did not already have. It moves what the body
had forward, and the entries say so in the same words each time: a creature under one of
them gains no Exhaustion level from going without sleep. That clause defers the Exhaustion
rather than cancelling it. The hours a creature did not sleep are still hours it did not
sleep, and they are counted when the simple ends.

<Note>
  When a simple that suspended the Exhaustion of going without sleep ends, the creature gains 1
  Exhaustion level for each Long Rest it missed while under it. That Exhaustion is removed by rest
  in the ordinary way, and there is no faster route to it below the fifth rung.
</Note>

A creature that takes kindleseed at dusk and works through one night has missed one Long
Rest; it gains 1 Exhaustion level when the 6 hours are up, sleeps, and the level goes. A
creature that takes nightsmith for four nights running has missed four, and gains all 4
levels at once when the last dose ends. A creature dies at 6, so the sixth consecutive
night is the one to count, and what kills is the missed sleep rather than the simple. This
is ordinary Exhaustion, and a run of Long Rests removes it.

The quieting family comes down differently. What returns is pain rather than tiredness, and
it returns at once rather than over an hour, because the simple was covering it rather than
treating it. A creature that spent 6 hours under hushcap has, at the end of the sixth,
exactly the injuries it had at the start plus whatever it did to itself in between, and it
will have done more than it otherwise would, because hushcap is taken to stop a body
reporting injury. This is not a new rule. It is the entry's own duration running out.

Treatment for either comedown is the first rung and the second: sleep, food and warmth, for
as long as it takes. Nothing on the bench shortens either. A waking simple given to a
creature coming down off a quieting one does not cancel the quieting. Both run at once,
each on its own duration, and the practitioner has two cases instead of one.

## The wanting

Craving is the count kept in [the first chapter](/strong-waters/chapter-1/): it rises by
what each entry names, it falls by 1 for each full day a creature goes without a measure,
and at 6 it converts to an addiction and resets to nothing. Everything a practitioner can
do before that conversion is done in days, and attendance is the only lever.

A day of attendance lowers Craving by 2 rather than 1, which halves the time: a creature at
5 Craving is three attended days from clear and six unattended days from the same place.

The fall only applies on a day the creature goes entirely without. A creature taking a
measure daily never has a falling day at all, so every entry accumulates at its full rate —
six days to an addiction on drover's cud, which adds 1, and two nights on nightsmith, which
adds 3. The count rises until something stops it.

## Addiction, and how it ends

The cure is abstinence, and there is no other. An addiction ends the way the first chapter
sets it out: a saving throw at the end of each day the creature goes without a measure —
Constitution for the first 3 days and Wisdom from the fourth — with successes equal to 1
plus its degree, at a Difficulty Class of 15 on the first day that falls by 1 for each
consecutive day after it. A creature at the first degree needs two
and can go the days without much trouble. A creature at the third needs four, and is the
one least able to stay away from the substance long enough to make them, which is the
difficulty this section is about.

The Difficulty Class falls because the first days are the worst and each day after is
easier than the one before. What does not get easier is the substance being in the room.
Taking a measure at any point sends the successes back to none and the Difficulty Class
back to 15, and after the addiction has ended a single measure restores it whole at the
degree it left off, so a practitioner's work continues after a creature is well.

An addiction does not kill. Withdrawal from anything in this book is one bad day repeated —
Disadvantage on Wisdom and Charisma checks throughout, and from the second degree 1
Exhaustion level that does not lift while the addiction lasts and does not accumulate. A
creature a fortnight into going without has that one level and no more. It is miserable and
impaired, and in no danger from the withdrawal itself.

The danger is the third degree's excess, which each entry names: the money spent, the night
not slept, the cup that ends a bad day and is followed by the second cup that kills. A
practitioner treating an addiction is treating the excess, and the treatments below are
aimed at it rather than at the count.

- **Attendance.** A creature attended for the whole of a day makes that day's saving throw
  with Advantage, and — because attendance means a day spent out of reach of the substance
  — is not called on to refuse one at all. The second effect matters more. A creature at
  the third degree has to refuse every time a measure is in the room, so the practitioner's
  work is emptying the room rather than improving the odds inside it.
- **The taper.** A measure taken under a practitioner's hand — on a day set aside for it,
  at a dose the practitioner is reducing — costs that day's saving throw, because the day
  was not gone without, but does not undo the successes already made and does not return
  the Difficulty Class to 15. It is the one exception to the rule above. A measure taken
  any other way is a relapse. The same measure out of the same bottle counts differently
  according to whether a practitioner is reducing the dose.
- **What magic does here.** Greater Restoration removes the Exhaustion level a second- or
  third-degree addiction lays down. That makes one day bearable and does nothing else: the
  level returns the next day the creature goes without, the saving throws are unchanged,
  and no spell in the Basic Rules reaches the addiction itself.

Nightsmith is the case the sixth chapter said would be set out here, and every number in
it comes from that entry. A measure adds 3 to Craving, so a scholar taking one a night is
at 6 on the second and Addicted, and at the third and deepest degree by the sixth. He
notices none of it, because every degree describes a day without a measure and there are no
such days while the work lasts. The fortnight it buys is real, and the work gets done.

When the work stops he has four saving throws to make, the first at DC 15 and each of them
easier than the last, a bad day every day he goes without, and 1 Exhaustion level that will
not lift until he takes a measure or finishes. He also has nightsmith's excess, which is
work: he takes a measure before anything he cares about doing, and has come to believe he
cannot do it otherwise. A scholar who believes that will not make four saving throws in a
row on his own, which is what attendance is for. The next commission he accepts, a year
later, brings the relapse rule into play.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  People ask me for something that ends the wanting, and I tell them what I have: a bed, a fortnight
  of my attention, and a bitter fortnight it is. They are disappointed, and then they ask whether
  there is a spell, and I tell them there is not, and they are disappointed again in the same
  quarter of an hour.

What there is, is somebody sitting up. I have not found a way to sell that, and I have stopped
trying to.

</Voice>

## The overdose

An overdose is measured against the entry's own threshold — the third measure, the second
cup — and what a practitioner can do about one divides at the entry's own Onset. Both
numbers are printed in the catalog's tables for this reason.

### Before onset

Before a simple takes hold, a swallowed measure is still in the stomach and can be brought
back up. A creature made to vomit before the Onset in the entry has passed has not taken
that measure at all: no effect, no overdose saving throw, and nothing added to Craving. The
emetic is salt water, or a strong infusion of mustard, and it works within a minute of
going down.

This rung is narrower than it looks. It does nothing for a measure that was smoked or
rubbed in, neither of which goes through the stomach, and nothing for one whose Onset has
already passed. Onsets in the sixth chapter run from immediate to an hour, so whether the
rung is available at all is decided by which simple was taken, before the practitioner
arrives.

### After onset

Once a simple has taken hold, charcoal is the bench's answer: hardwood burnt, ground fine,
and given in water. A creature that swallows it within 10 minutes of a measure makes that
measure's overdose saving throw with Advantage. The charcoal is inert in itself and does no
harm at any dose, which is why it is kept made up in a jar rather than prepared when it is
wanted — the 10 minutes do not allow for preparing it.

### When the breath goes

Milk poppy, sister's ease and sleepwrack stop a body breathing. A creature dying this way
is not wounded, there is nothing to stanch, and nothing on the first three rungs reaches
it. The fourth rung is the whole treatment, and it requires a person rather than a
preparation.

<Note>
  A creature that spends its whole turn working the chest and breath of a creature dying from a
  simple suspends that creature's death saving throws: while the work continues without a break, the
  dying creature neither improves nor worsens, and it resumes exactly where it left off the moment
  the work stops. This keeps a creature alive. It does not wake it, and it does not treat what is in
  it.
</Note>

The work is not a cure. It holds a creature where it is until the overdose passes on its
own or until a slot arrives, and it occupies whoever is doing it completely for as long as
it lasts.

### What cannot be caught

Four entries in the sixth chapter kill outright rather than by degrees, and each says so
in its own entry. Three of them — milk poppy, sister's ease and sleepwrack — kill by
stopping the breath, and the rung above is what answers them. The fourth does not.
Nightsmith's third measure gives no window at all: the saving throw is the whole of the
event, a failure is a death, and there is nothing before it for anybody to catch.

Where a creature does die, Revivify reaches back 1 minute. A practitioner who has been
working somebody's chest for 20 minutes is 19 minutes outside that window, so the spell
answers a death that happens in front of a caster and not one that happens while somebody
goes to find one.

<Voice title="Of what to do at three in the morning" by="Hesper Aldwine">
  One will find out what was taken, and how much, and when, and one will ask the people who carried
  them in rather than the one lying down.

If it is not yet in them, one will bring it up. If it is, one will give charcoal in water. If the
breath has gone, one will breathe for them and one will go on breathing for them, and one will
send the fastest person in the room for anybody with a slot to spend.

And one will not give a waking simple to a body that has stopped breathing, whatever one has been
told by whoever is shouting. It does not answer the quieting. It only means there are two things
in them.

</Voice>

## What magic does, and where it stops

Magic reaches this craft in three places: what is in the cup, a short list of named
conditions, and telling one prepared thing from another. In all three it stops at the same
line. A spell can destroy a dose, and it can end a condition a dose caused, and it cannot
reach the fact that a body has taken one — not the Exhaustion, not the Craving, not the
addiction, and not a creature whose breathing has stopped. The rulings below follow what
the spells in the Basic Rules actually say.

### Purify Food and Drink

The spell takes the harm and the virtue together, because in everything in this book they
are the same substance: there is no part of a simple that does the good and some other part
that does the damage, so a spell that removes what is harmful removes the preparation. It
works every time and needs no check, and what it leaves behind is inert:

- Cast on a plant, growing or gathered, it is no longer that plant. The leaf is safe and
  worth nothing, and nothing can be prepared from it.
- Cast on a prepared dose, the dose is harmless. It is also inert — no measure, no effect,
  and nothing added to Craving.
- Cast on a strong water, the drink goes out of it. What remains is water, or tea, or wort:
  safe, drinkable, and worth no Intoxication at all.
- Cast on what has already been swallowed, it does nothing, because the spell has food and
  drink for its target and never a creature.

The last of those is the one the rest of this chapter rests on. A 1st-level spell slot
destroys the cup and cannot touch the person who has already drunk from it, and every rung
of the ladder below the fifth exists because of that.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  It is a fine spell and I have used it on a barrel that came back from a wedding, and there is
  nothing wrong with it that is not in the reading. It does not take the harm out and leave the
  good. There is no seam between them to cut along. What one has afterward is a clean cup of
  nothing, which is a fair price for a barrel and a ruinous one for a month's tincturing.

And it will not chase a thing that has been drunk. I have watched a young man of the cloth cast it
four times over a woman who had taken milk poppy, at a shilling a slot by his own accounting, and
what saved her was a girl of eleven holding her mouth open.

</Voice>

### The restorations, and what they miss

Three spells end the Poisoned condition, which is the consequence a good many entries in
the sixth chapter name, and that is the whole of what they do here:

- Lesser Restoration ends Poisoned, as a Bonus Action, on a touch. Against this catalog it
  answers emberfoot's third measure, larkwort's overdose, and a second measure of dulcet
  root, milk poppy or nightsmith — all of which name the Poisoned condition and nothing
  else.
- Heal ends Poisoned as well, alongside its healing, and is worth naming here only because
  a creature that has overdosed is frequently also hurt.
- Protection from Poison ends Poisoned and, for 1 hour, gives Advantage on saving throws to
  avoid or end it. Against this catalog that Advantage applies where an entry's overdose
  names the Poisoned condition as its consequence, and nowhere else, because every other
  overdose calls for a saving throw against damage, Exhaustion, unconsciousness or death
  rather than against being Poisoned. Its Resistance to Poison damage is worth nothing here
  at all: nothing in the sixth chapter deals Poison damage. What these simples do, they do
  by driving a body past what it will take, so the entries that deal damage deal Psychic
  and Necrotic.

None of the three touches an Exhaustion level, the Unconscious condition, a Craving, an
addiction, or a creature that has stopped breathing. Greater Restoration removes 1
Exhaustion level, which is one day of an addiction and no part of the addiction itself.

Detect Poison and Disease is the spell a practitioner is most often offered for diagnosis,
and it answers a different question than the one being asked. It finds prepared measures
within 30 feet and names what each of them is, which settles what is in a bottle, a cup, or
on a shelf. It does not read a creature that has already swallowed one: a dosed creature is
neither a poison nor a poisonous creature, so the spell has nothing in it to find. The
bottle can be identified by a spell and the person cannot, and what a creature has taken is
established by asking — the creature, or whoever carried it in, as the first chapter says.

The remedies in the eighth chapter were written for what is left after all of the above: the
craving, the comedown, the overdose still in progress, and the hands that will not steady.
