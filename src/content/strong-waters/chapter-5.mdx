A weak simple is a preparation that looks like something in the sixth chapter and is not.
Rosemary and mint rolled and sold as smallweed. Chalk and flour sold as a reduction that
cost eight gold. A spent poppy head sold as the milk that was taken out of it a week ago.

They are here for two reasons. A practitioner meets far more of them than of the real
thing, and the treatment for a creature that has taken one is not the treatment for a
creature that has taken what it paid for. And a creature that took one and believes
otherwise is in a condition of its own, which this chapter sets out.

Nothing here is sold as itself. That is what makes it a weak simple rather than a herb.

## What one does

A creature that takes a weak simple believing it to be the thing it imitates gains that
thing's effect for 10 minutes, and no part of it afterward. A creature that knows what it
is gains nothing at all.

The effect is the imitated entry's first measure only. A second measure does nothing, no
threshold is ever reached, and none of the imitated entry's later clauses apply. What a
creature has is the shape of the thing without the substance under it, and the shape lasts
about as long as attention does.

## What one does not do

Nothing in this chapter has an overdose, a threshold, or a measure past which a saving
throw decides anything. No quantity of any of it kills a creature, poisons it, or costs it
an Exhaustion level. All four of the entries in the sixth chapter that kill are imitated
here, and not one of the imitations can.

This is the whole of the good news, and it is worth putting beside the rule at the bottom
of the sixth chapter's ladder: what is bought below a poor house is not reliably a measure.
Sometimes that is how a creature dies, and sometimes it is the only reason one did not.

## The count still rises

Craving rises at the rate of the simple being imitated, for any creature that believes it
took the real thing.

A body learns a habit from the hour, the ritual and the expectation, and none of the three
requires the substance to have been present. A creature that smokes rosemary every evening
for a fortnight, in the belief that it is smallweed, arrives at the same count on the same
day as a creature that smoked smallweed, and its degrees arrive on schedule and behave
exactly as the first chapter describes.

So a creature can reach the third degree of an addiction to something it has never taken.
She records four such cases and says they were the hardest of her working life, because
there was nothing in the patient to treat.

## How one ends

An addiction to a weak simple ends when the creature is shown, plainly and with the thing
in front of it, what it has been taking. The jar opened, the herb named, the same leaf
burned beside it for comparison. There are no saving throws and no days gone without.

It is the only addiction in this book that ends by being explained, and it is why the tells
below are worth learning rather than merely interesting. It is also why a practitioner
does not say it kindly or by halves: a creature half-convinced is a creature still
addicted.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  I have cured three men in an afternoon by opening a jar, and each of them was angry with me for a
  year afterward, which I had not expected the first time and expected thoroughly by the third.

There is no way to do it gently. A thing either is what it was sold as or it is not, and a man who
has spent four months of wages on rosemary would rather be ill than be a fool. I tell them anyway.
I have not found the other way.

</Voice>

## Telling them apart

Every entry names its tell, and the tells are the useful part of this chapter. Most of them
are a smell or an ash rather than a taste, because a practitioner is rarely offered a taste
and is frequently offered a jar.

A creature that has read this book, or that has an apothecary's training, finds the tell
without a check. Anybody else who examines the goods carefully and knows what to look for
may need one, and the harder imitations are the ones prepared by somebody who has also read
a book.

| Weak simple   | Sold as       | What it is                            | The tell                                               |
| ------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Hearthsmoke   | Smallweed     | Rosemary, mint and sage, rolled       | Smells of a kitchen. The ash is white, not brown       |
| Chalkroot     | Emberfoot     | Chalk, flour and ground pepper        | No sourness at all, and it settles out of the cup      |
| Falsecap      | Hushcap       | A field mushroom dried hard           | Browns only at the edge; hushcap is fawn to the stem   |
| Beetwater     | Quickthorn    | Beet juice in cheap spirit            | Smells of earth. Quickthorn smells of almonds          |
| Poppy husk    | Milk poppy    | The spent head, after the milk is out | Bitter but thin, and it does not cling to the glass    |
| Nightsoot     | Nightsmith    | Soot, yeast and salt                  | Washes out of the cup. Nightsmith never does           |
| Cordial drops | Sister's ease | Sugar, spirit and one drop of bitter  | Thin and clear held to a light; the true is near black |

Each is sold at the price of the thing it imitates, and each is worth a few coppers. That
gap is the entire trade.

Three of them are worth more than a line.

### Hearthsmoke

The commonest weak simple in the book, and the one a practitioner meets weekly. Rosemary,
mint and sage dried and rolled in the same papers smallweed is rolled in, by somebody who
has watched the real thing being made.

It is genuinely pleasant to smoke, which is why it sells to people who have never had
smallweed and cannot be disappointed by it. Those creatures take no Craving at all, having
believed nothing, and simply enjoy an evening.

### Poppy husk

The one entry here that is not inert. The head has been scored and the milk taken, and what
is left holds a little of what it held before — enough to be real and nowhere near enough to
be milk poppy.

A creature that takes a measure ignores the effects of one Exhaustion level for 1 hour,
whether it believes anything or not. That is the whole of it. It has no overdose, and a
creature cannot take enough of it to stop breathing, which is the difference that matters.

Craving rises at milk poppy's rate of 4 for a creature that believes it took milk poppy,
and at 1 for a creature that knows exactly what it has.

### Cordial drops

Sugar, spirit and a drop of something bitter, sold in the small dark bottle sister's ease is
sold in, at sister's ease's price of 120 gp.

It is the most profitable object in this book and the most frequently fatal by omission: a
creature carrying it as its emergency against pain has nothing, and finds out in the hour it
mattered. It is also the reason a number of creatures who took what they believed was a
second drop are still alive.

<Voice by="Hesper Aldwine">
  I have been asked whether I would sell a false cordial drop to a man I judged likely to take the
  real one to its end, and I will not answer it in a book that anybody may read. I will say that the
  question has been put to me twice, by people who had thought about it longer than I had.
</Voice>
