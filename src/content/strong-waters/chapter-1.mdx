Hesper Aldwine kept a shop on the wet side of a river town for something over fifty years,
and died there. She was a druid, though she seldom said so and appears to have thought
little of the fact; what she was in practice was an apothecary, and what she left behind
was a working book — the notes of a woman who prepared these things, sold them, and then
treated the people who had bought them.

It was never published. It was copied, and copied from the copies, which is why no two
editions agree on the order of the chapters and why the marginal notes in yours may not be
hers. What follows is as close to her hand as anyone has managed.

## Who she wrote for

Not for patients. She assumed a reader who could tell one leaf from another, keep a fire at
a given heat for four hours, and be trusted with the answer to "how much is too much" —
because that answer is in here for every substance she cataloged, plainly, with the
numbers.

She is not moralizing anywhere in this book, with one exception, and a reader looking for
her disapproval anywhere else will be disappointed. The exception is water. She holds, and
states in four separate places, that water drunk on its own rusts the bones, and no
argument or evidence appears to have been brought against it in fifty years of practice. A thing that helps and harms is simply a thing that helps and harms, and
she cataloged it the same way she cataloged the ones that only help. Her position, stated
once in a margin and never again, was that people will drink and people will dose
themselves, and the only question a practitioner faces is whether they will do it knowing
what it costs.

## How a receipt is read

Every entry in the two catalogs is laid out the same way.

It opens with what the thing is: where it grows or who makes it, and how a practitioner
knows it — by sight, by smell, and by taste, in that order, because that is the order in
which one meets it.

Then her preparing, set apart, in her own words. This is the one part of the book where she
speaks directly, and it is worth reading even for a substance you will never prepare; she
is frequently useful and occasionally very funny about people who have wasted her time.

Then what it does. A **measure** is her unit throughout — one dose, defined per entry,
because a mouthful of one thing is not a mouthful of another. The entries say what one
measure does, what a second does on top of it, and where the line is past which a
Constitution saving throw decides the matter. Where a substance is habit-forming she gives
what it adds to **Craving**, which is the count she kept of how much a body has learned to
want a thing.

She uses spells sparingly and names them by their common names. Where a spell already does
what a step of a receipt does, she says so and does not write a second one; where a spell
is commonly claimed to serve and does not, she says that too, usually at length.

## The count she kept

Craving runs across both catalogs. Everything in this book that a body learns to want
carries it and it behaves the same way for all of them.

A **Craving** is a count kept for one creature and one substance. Each entry says what it
adds. The count falls by 1 for each full day a creature goes entirely without — without any
measure of the thing at all, and not merely without enough of it to feel. A creature taking
something every day never falls at all, however little it takes.

It is counted per substance: a body that wants one thing does not want another on that
account, however alike the two look from outside. There are two exceptions, and both exist
because a body genuinely cannot tell the difference — smallweed and goldleaf share one
count, and all the drink in the third chapter shares one between the whole of it.

At 6 the count converts. The creature becomes **Addicted** to that substance at the first
degree and the count resets to 0. It then runs on as before, and each further time it
reaches 6 the addiction deepens by one degree, to a maximum of the third. So 6 in total
brings a creature to the first degree, 12 to the second and 18 to the third, however many
days each of those takes to arrive.

### What moves what

Three things are counted in this book, and they are not the same kind of thing. Intoxication
is a condition that arrives and goes in an evening. Craving is a count that moves over days.
An addiction is a state that does not move at all once it has arrived.

|                  | What moves it                                                                       | What it does                           | How it goes                                     |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **Intoxication** | A measure past capacity, on a failed Constitution saving throw. Drink only          | Four levels, one at a time             | Falls 1 an hour without drinking                |
| **Craving**      | Drink: a day that reached the third or fourth level. A simple: what its entry names | Nothing at all until it reaches 6      | Falls 1 for each full day gone entirely without |
| **Addiction**    | Every further 6 of Craving, to a maximum of the third degree                        | Three degrees, on any day gone without | Only by going without, and never completely     |

The chain runs one way. Drinking does nothing until it passes capacity; Intoxication adds
nothing to the count until it reaches the third level; the count does nothing until it
reaches 6. A creature can drink every evening of its life and never enter the second column,
provided it stops before the third level.

### When any of it is worth keeping

She kept a column for perhaps one customer in twenty, and the arithmetic is the reason.

A Craving that never reaches 6 does nothing whatever, and the count falls by 1 on every day
between. A creature drunk at a wedding and again at a funeral is at 2 in the spring and 0 by
the summer. Two nights in a season, or four, or one a month, never arrive anywhere — there
is nothing there to write down, and writing it down will only make a practitioner believe
something is happening.

What moves is a creature taking something more days than it does not: the soldier with a
flask, the scholar on a commission, the customer who has begun to arrange the day around it.
That is the only case in which a count climbs faster than it slides, and it is the only case
in which 6 arrives. An evening of Intoxication is worth remembering until the morning; a
Craving is worth writing down once a creature has strung days together; an addiction is
worth keeping written down for good, because a creature that recovers and takes one measure
years later returns to the degree it left.

### The three degrees

The effects of a degree are cumulative and stack together. What they describe is a day on which the
creature goes without:

1. **The habit.** The creature has Disadvantage on ability checks based on Wisdom and Charisma. It
   can go the day without, and would rather not.
2. **The need.** The creature gains 1 Exhaustion level, which no Long Rest removes while
   the addiction lasts and which taking a measure removes at once. Where another creature
   presses a measure on it, it must make the saving throw below to refuse, and makes it
   with Disadvantage.
3. **The ruin.** A measure present at all calls that saving throw — offered, sitting on a
   table, or its own and in a cupboard. On a failure the creature takes one, and takes the
   entry's excess with it.

That Exhaustion level does not accumulate. A creature ten days into going without has 1
level from its addiction and not 10, and the day it takes a measure the level goes.
Withdrawal from anything in this book is a very bad day, repeated for as long as it lasts,
and it is not a death.

An entry with an excess names it in its own text. It is the thing a creature at the third
degree does that it would not otherwise do — the money it spends, the sleep it will not
take, the cup it ends every bad day with — and it is where the harm of an addiction
actually falls. Where an entry names none, the creature simply always has some.

### The saving throw

One saving throw serves an addiction, and it is made in two situations: to refuse a measure
that is in front of the creature, and at the end of each day gone without, to be free of
the addiction altogether. Both use the same Difficulty Class and the same ability.

The **Difficulty Class** is 15 on the first day the creature has gone without, and falls by
1 for each consecutive day after that, to a minimum of 5. It falls because refusing gets
easier the longer it has been, which is the one thing everybody who has done it agrees on.
Taking a measure returns it to 15.

The **ability** depends on how long it has been, because the wanting moves. For the first 3
days it is a **Constitution** saving throw: the body is doing the wanting, and it wants
loudly. From the fourth day it is a **Wisdom** saving throw, because by then the body has
largely finished and what is left is the creature's own memory of the thing. That is why
creatures relapse after years and not after a fortnight — the part that lasts was never in
the body. Where a case sits on the line, the Game Master decides which the day belongs to.

A creature at the second degree makes this saving throw only when somebody presses a
measure on it, and makes it with Disadvantage. A creature at the third makes it whenever a
measure is anywhere within reach, and with Disadvantage only if somebody is pressing. Both
are made carrying whatever Exhaustion the addiction has laid down, which is the point: it
is hardest on the days the creature is worst.

### Reading the degree

Where a count has been kept, the degree is settled by arithmetic and there is nothing to
decide. Most creatures a practitioner meets have had nobody counting, and for them the
degree is read off what the creature does — which is what the descriptions in the entries
are for. A creature that wants one every day and is poor company without it is at the
first. A creature that has stopped choosing is at the third.

No spell reads it. It is read by watching, and by asking the people a creature lives with
rather than the creature, which is why the third of the three things she asks below is the
one she gives twice.

### How it ends

An addiction ends by going without, and by nothing else. At the end of each day a creature
goes without a measure it makes the saving throw above, and it needs successes equal to 1
plus its degree to be free of it — two at the first degree, three at the second, four at
the third.

A failure does not undo a success. Taking a measure does: the successes go to none, and the
Difficulty Class goes back to 15 with them, which is why the days a creature is nearly
through are the expensive ones to lose.

An addiction that has ended is not undone. A creature that was Addicted and takes a measure
again is Addicted again at once, at the degree it left off, without passing back through
the count — and it does not matter how long it has been, whether a season or thirty years.
This is the one thing in the book that does not lapse with time, and it is why she writes
the degree down and keeps it written down after a creature is well.

The treatments in the fifth chapter make those days survivable and shorten them
considerably. Nothing removes an addiction in an afternoon, and nothing removes the fact of
having had one at all.

## What she asks

Three things, from the front page of the oldest copy.

The first is that the numbers be kept. She measured them, over a working life, on people
who trusted her; a reader who halves a dose because it seems harsh has not made the
substance gentler, only their knowledge of it worse.

The second is that a preparation be finished or abandoned, never rushed. Most of what she
records going wrong went wrong because somebody was in a hurry, and the failures are not
the pleasant kind.

The third she wrote as a single line, and it is the only advice in the book she gives twice:
_know what the person in front of you has already taken._

## What this book assumes of a table

It is a book about things that help and harm, and the harm is written down as plainly as
the help — the doses, the comedowns, the wanting, and what happens to somebody who does not
stop. Some of it is grim, because the craft is.

None of it obliges anybody. A table that wants the drinking and none of the rest can take
the third chapter and leave the fourth on the shelf; a table that wants the whole weight of
it will find that written down too. The Game Master decides which book this is at their
table, and the catalog is indifferent either way.
