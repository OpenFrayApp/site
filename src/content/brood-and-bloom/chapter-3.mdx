This chapter is what happens when somebody reads the order's files and reaches the opposite
conclusion. It covers the cult that came out of that reading, the book it was founded on,
the rite that admits a member, and six creatures who are people first. The alchemy and the
spells both sides use are in [chapter 7](/brood-and-bloom/chapter-7/).

The Lazaret files the cult as the Apocrypha, after their book rather than after the people,
because the book came first and the people are an appendix to it. Its author was a lector
of the order who spent eleven years copying case records in the houses and drew from them
the opposite of everything the order teaches. He did not steal the files. He published,
exactly as doctrine requires, and what he published was an argument that a graft is not an
affliction but an offer. Then he walked out of his house and took a hundred and forty pages
with him.

His followers call themselves the Hands of the Host, and a member is a hand. Their argument
is that a body carrying a parasite is not diminished but occupied, that occupation is the
only company which does not end, and that a body with nothing in it is unfinished.

## The True File

The cult calls it the True File. The Lazaret was founded on a document, and everything the
order has written in the years since is a copy of that document, a comment on it, or an
addition to it. The book does not claim to come after it. It claims the order has spent its
entire existence keeping an accurate record of the wrong conclusion, and that the
correction was sitting in its own files the whole time, available to anybody willing to
read them in order.

The Lazaret gives the book a name of its own and will not use the author's. A file naming a
man invites the question of who else agreed with him, and the order would rather answer
questions about a document.

A hundred and forty pages in a lector's hand. No illumination, no decoration, ruled
margins, and a case number beside every record in the first part, because the man who
copied them out was trained to keep the reference and could not stop doing it. Those
numbers are the reason the order takes captured copies seriously. Each one leads back to a
house, a year, and a patient who was admitted, treated, and written up by somebody who
thought they were helping.

Every copy is made by hand, and copying is the closest thing the cult has to a third rite.
The work is given to hands whose grade has taken their legs but not their sight. A full
copy takes one of them most of a year.

| Part | Name             | Contents                                                                   |
| ---- | ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| I    | The records      | Eleven years of copied Lazaret case files, with their numbers intact       |
| II   | The argument     | The doctrine drawn from them: emptiness, the assignment, and leaving       |
| III  | The offices      | How a cell is run, who holds what, and the order of the morning            |
| IV   | The preparations | Countenance first, then every remedy the order knows, for all three broods |
| V    | The workings     | The spells                                                                 |

Parts I through III travel together, and a new cell is founded with them. Part IV arrives
when the cell has held its ground long enough to be worth supplying, which is a judgment
the First Graft makes personally and slowly. Part V arrives in his own hand or not at all.
He writes the workings out one at a time, for one cell at a time, and he has never
explained the criteria.

### Part IV

The preparations run longer than the other four parts together, and almost none of it is
original. The order publishes what it learns, so its treatments for all three broods sat in
the case files the author spent eleven years copying. He transcribed them accurately. It is
the single most useful thing the cult owns, and the reason an almoner's bag holds real
medicine rather than a prop.

A cell can therefore treat a sporing case competently, work a
[Spore Load](/brood-and-bloom/chapter-5/#spore-load) down, dress a rotgill wound, and hand
out the same formulas an officer of the order would hand out, at the same strength, for
nothing. It does this without hesitation and files it under hygiene rather than charity: a
brood that offers nothing to any individual is worth curing, and a cell cannot afford to
lose hands to one on the road.

The inquiline treatments are in part IV as well, transcribed as faithfully as everything
else and never used. Against each of them, in the author's hand, is a single word:
_Refused._

### Part V

A hand's spells are rare for reasons that have nothing to do with difficulty. Most cells
have never seen part V. Cells that have are old, dug in, and have been doing something for
years that persuaded a careful man in another place to write to them.

The Lazaret holds four copies, all incomplete, none with a part V. The counter-work at the
first house is built from them.

## What they believe

The True File does not claim the broods are gods. It claims they are the only living things
that have solved the problem every other living thing dies of, and that they are generous
with the solution. A hand does not pray to an inquiline: what the book describes is an offer
of a body, and the cult uses that word for it.

Three doctrines run through the book and appear in every cell, however far apart.

The first is that emptiness is a condition rather than a default. A body with nothing in it
is unfinished, and the discomfort people feel around the marked is recognition rather than
disgust.

The second is that the offer cannot be negotiated. What line a graft takes is decided at
the feeding and not by the one receiving it, so a hand asks for nothing specific and is
given what the parasite decides to give. The cult calls this the assignment. There is no
appeal and no second attempt.

The third is that leaving is worse than dying. A graft can be expelled, and the discipline
that ends one takes a day, which the True File cites as evidence that people do not value
what they have been given.

## The Kenotics

The rite is called the Kenotics, and it is the only ceremony the cult performs once.

A cell keeps latchkin. This is the practical fact underneath all the liturgy, and it is the
reason the Lazaret hunts cells the way it hunts colonies. A Kenotics requires a live
inquiline in a condition to feed, so every cell of any size maintains a
[clutch](/brood-and-bloom/chapter-4/#c-latchling-clutch), feeds it, and loses members to it
at a rate no cell records.

The postulant is brought in, held, and fed on once. The officiant is an ostiary. The
[graft](/brood-and-bloom/chapter-4/#the-graft) is left, the parasite detaches, and the
assignment is read from the marks over the following days. From that hour the postulant is
a hand, and the date is kept the way other people keep a birthday. Before the day is out
they are dosed with Countenance and taught to prepare it, because a hand who cannot be kept
working is of no use to a cell.

The assignment decides what the rest of a hand's life looks like.

A sallow assignment is fatal, and everyone present knows it when the marks come up. The
cult calls it being called early, treats the death as selection rather than misfortune, and
considers it the most complete of the three outcomes. A hand who draws sallow is
congratulated.

A chantry assignment is the one hands hope for and will not admit to hoping for. Every
spell the host spends deepens the graft, so a caster advances faster than anyone else in
the cell, and the discipline that would expel it is a single day spent casting nothing. A
chantry hand therefore casts daily, without exception, as an act of devotion, which makes
the cult's spellcasters both its most advanced members and its least stable.

A palimpsest assignment takes memory, a piece at a time, without announcement. The cult
calls it being unwritten and considers it the cleanest of the three, on the grounds that a
hand who has forgotten what they were before cannot be argued back into it.

## Ranks and offices

Two ladders run at once. Grade is how deep the graft has gone. Office is what a hand does,
and most of the offices are work that requires walking, talking, lifting, and being trusted
near the living, all of which a graft takes away in that order. The exception is the
sacristan, which can be done sitting down and is therefore the only office a deep grade can
hold.

Nobody holds an office as a promotion. A hand is given one because they are still able to
perform it, and loses it the moment they are not.

| Rank            | Ladder | Meaning                                                             |
| --------------- | ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Almoner         | Office | Uninfected. Recruits, and buys the Kenotics by bringing one in      |
| Sexton          | Office | Keeps the ground, and lays out the dead who go into it. Stage 1     |
| Ostiary         | Office | The doorkeeper. Performs the Kenotics and keeps the clutch. Stage 2 |
| Sacristan       | Office | Prepares and issues the cell's Countenance. Stage 3                 |
| Postulant       | Grade  | Stage 1. Has asked and been given, and nothing has moved yet        |
| Communicant     | Grade  | Stage 2. Has received, and can feel it                              |
| Ciborium        | Grade  | Stage 3. The vessel that holds the host                             |
| Translation     | Grade  | Stage 4. Not a rank. What the cult calls the end of a case          |
| The First Graft | Head   | The author. Above both ladders, and held at stage 3                 |

Every one of them is a person, and a cell is not a warband. Almoners work alone and in the
open, sextons work at night and at a distance, ostiaries stay with the clutch, and
sacristans do not leave the room they brew in. Hands fight to protect the clutch and rarely
for any other reason. They surrender readily, talk freely, and treat capture as an
opportunity to explain the book.

The cult is written for slow discovery rather than ambush. An almoner met early and liked
does more for the chapter than any encounter that opens with a drawn weapon, and a party
that accepts help from one before learning what they are has already been in reach of the
clutch.

Every hand except the almoner carries a graft and is under Countenance when the party meets
them, which is why none of these blocks shows the penalties of a stage. An almoner carries
nothing at all and examines healthy by any means. A hand caught before Matins is a
different creature, and the Grafted trait on each block says what changes.

### Almoners

Almoners are not hands. An almoner is clean, has never been fed on, and cannot be given the
Kenotics until they have brought somebody else in first. One delivered candidate buys the
rite, and nothing else does. The cult treats this as the only loyalty test it needs, on the
grounds that a person who has handed over a stranger has already accepted the book's
argument in practice.

They arrive during outbreaks, in bad seasons, and wherever a house is admitting more than
it can hold. They carry real medicine and real food and give it away without conditions,
and they do not say who sent them. At a distance a village cannot tell an almoner from an
officer of the Lazaret. Up close it still cannot, because there is nothing under the skin
to find. The resemblance is deliberate and carefully maintained, and it is what gets an
almoner through a gate.

<Creature name="Almoner">
  **Running it.** An almoner does not fight until there is no version of the conversation left, and
  nothing in the block is built to hurt anybody. The pressure worth playing is the one they are
  under: an almoner cannot be given the rite until they have delivered somebody, and a friendly
  party traveling toward somewhere isolated is the opportunity they have been waiting on. One who
  succeeds becomes a postulant, and the postulant block is what they are the following week.
</Creature>

### Postulants

A postulant is a hand in their first weeks: fed on once, carrying a graft that has not
moved, and dosed with Countenance from the day of the rite. The grade is defined by what
has not happened yet. Nothing shows, nothing hurts, and the assignment read from the marks
is the only thing about them that has been settled.

Cells give postulants the work that needs a body rather than a judgment — digging,
carrying, standing at doors, walking a measure out to a sexton at the far edge of the
ground. They are not trusted with the clutch, they are not told who the sacristan is, and
they are rarely sent to speak to anyone the cult wants. A postulant recruited by an almoner
has often not met a second hand yet.

Most of them are still being looked for. The cult treats this as an ordinary risk rather
than a secret to keep: a postulant writes home in their own hand, saying they have taken
work and will be some time, and an ostiary reads each letter before it goes.

The grade is also where the cult loses most of the people it takes. A Kenotics that does
not root leaves a postulant clean, which the book counts as a failure rather than a
reprieve, and the second offer covers them like any other.

<Creature name="Postulant">
  **Running it.** Postulants dig, carry, and hold doors, and a party usually meets them doing
  exactly that. Offer is a plea rather than a spell: it works on people who have not yet decided
  what the cult is, and a character who has watched a hand die makes the save with Advantage. A
  postulant met before Matins carries the penalties of stage 1 and knows how long it is until the
  queue forms.
</Creature>

### Sextons

Sextons are the opposite office, and the newest hands hold it. A postulant is given the
ground before any contact with anyone the cult hopes to recruit, on the reasoning that a
hand who will be going into that ground eventually should learn it while they can still
dig. A sexton knows to the hour how long a body stays worth carrying, and treats arriving
late as a personal failure rather than an accident. The office also carries out the second
offer, which is why cells give it to hands too new to have made friends.

<Creature name="Sexton">
  **Running it.** A sexton is not trying to kill the party. It is trying to get a body to the ground
  before the day is out, and it treats a fight as an obstruction. Hook drags a target into reach,
  Consign takes them off their feet and keeps them there, and Second Offer collects whatever the
  fight produces. One that has hold of a body does not let go to fight anybody else — it drags, and
  it heads for the ground it keeps.
</Creature>

### Ostiaries

The doorkeeper performs the Kenotics, keeps the clutch fed, and decides who is admitted. In
a cell of any size the ostiary is also the only hand who knows every other hand by name,
which makes the office the cult's memory and its weakest point.

When measures run short the ostiary decides who is passed over, and there is no doctrine
covering the decision. Cells that have gone short more than once are difficult to approach
and impossible to negotiate with.

<Creature name="Ostiary">
  **Running it.** An ostiary fights to keep the party out of two rooms and will trade anything else.
  Bar the Door spends a Lazaret Sill on the approach rather than on the fight, and Assumption of the
  Case is the tell: an ostiary casting it is pulling Depth off a hand who has gone down, in front of
  the party and without embarrassment. One who has decided the cell is lost spends their last action
  opening the clutch rather than closing it.
</Creature>

### Ciboria and sacristans

A Ciborium is a hand at stage 3, named for the vessel that holds the host. The graft stands
up under the skin from wrist to collarbone, the legs have mostly stopped being useful, and
what is left is a person who can still think, still speak, and still work with their hands.

That is the office of sacristan. It requires no walking, no lifting, and no contact with
anyone outside the cell, so it falls to a Ciborium who has nothing else left to do. They
keep the clutch, harvest it, brew the day's measures, and count them.

The quarters sit as deep inside a cell's holding as the building allows, behind whatever
the cell can put between them and a door. At the first house of the cult they adjoin the
First Graft's own rooms and share a wall.

Who the sacristan is, is not general knowledge. Most hands never learn, and the ones who do
are the ostiary and whoever carries food in. A sacristan issues measures through a hatch or
a curtain, robed and hooded, with the hands gloved and the arms covered, so that not even
the marks are visible to somebody standing a foot away. A cell that loses its sacristan to
a raid frequently does not know which of its dead it has lost until the following morning.

Taking the sacristy ends a cell faster than fighting it. Every hand in the place is
symptomatic by the following morning, and stage 4 arrives on schedule regardless.

<Creature name="Ciborium">
  **Running it.** A ciborium is found in a room, seated, behind whatever the cell could put in the
  way. Choose the line of its graft when you place it, because Seed hands out that line and nothing
  else. The Depth count is the fight: Seed and Graft spend it, Vessel and Hold take it back, and it
  will not cross 5. It opens at 3 Depth, which is one Seed and nothing more, so everything after
  that has to come off a body standing near it — its own hands are the obvious supply, and a party
  that has just been seeded is the better one. Cornered at 5 Depth with nobody grafted in reach, it
  stops spending and works with the cane.
</Creature>

### Translation

Translation is the word for what the order would file as a
[stage 4](/brood-and-bloom/chapter-1/#stage-4) case. In church usage it means both the
moving of a saint's relics and being taken up without dying, and the cult means both. A
cell that has produced a Translation does not mourn and does not disband. It moves, because
whatever the case became is still nearby and still feeding.

## Countenance

Countenance is the cult's only universal possession. Without it a cell would consist of
people who cannot walk, work, or pass for well, and the True File spends more pages on its
preparation than on anything the cult believes.

A dose is a measure. It is brewed rather than enchanted, which is the reason the cult
functions at all: a hand needs no magic to make one, carry one, or take one. A hand who can
already cast is taught the spell instead and stops queuing for the sacristy, though they do
not stop attending. Both last 24 hours and both are taken at first light. The rules for the
measure are under [Draughts](/brood-and-bloom/chapter-7/#draughts) and for the spell under
[Magic](/brood-and-bloom/chapter-7/#s-countenance).

A hand under Countenance feels well, looks well, works a full day, and passes any
examination that does not involve magic. Underneath it the graft advances exactly as it
would in somebody screaming. The Lazaret's catalog files it as symptomatic suppression and
marks it the most dangerous entry in the book, on the grounds that it does no harm whatever
and hides everything. No house stocks it and no house will make one.

### Matins

A measure lasts 24 hours and is taken at first light, before food, before work, before
anything. The cult calls the observance Matins after the first office of the day, and it is
the only ceremony a hand performs more than once.

The timing is not devotional in origin. A measure taken at first light lapses at first
light, so every hand in a cell wakes symptomatic and stays that way until they reach the
front of the queue. The line therefore forms in grade order, deepest first: a Postulant can
stand and wait for an hour, and a Ciborium cannot.

Matins is a queue rather than a service. The cell forms up at the sacristy, each hand takes
their measure at the hatch, and each drinks it there and is seen to. Hands who can cast do
not skip the line. They come to the same place at the same hour and cast in front of the
sacristan instead of drinking. The observance is a roll call as much as a distribution, and
a cell learns who is missing before it learns anything else that morning.

Nobody carries their own except almoners on the road and sextons working ground at a
distance. A hand who has missed a morning cannot hide it, and a hand who has left cannot
get another.

Hands who never go outside take their measure the same as anyone. The penalties of a stage
are not a matter of appearances, and a sacristan without a measure cannot hold a pestle.
The most housebound members of a cell are its most dependent, which includes the sacristan
and the First Graft.

### The objection

If a graft is a gift, taking something every morning to blunt it is difficult to defend,
and the True File knows it. The book's answer is that the symptoms are not the gift. They
are the body's objection to the gift, produced by an organism that has spent its whole life
alone and cannot be expected to know better. The measure is therefore not a refusal of what
was given, but a treatment of the complaint about it.

Not every hand accepts this. The ones who refuse the measure are called the Unmeasured, and
they are the only members of the cult who are unambiguously admired. They also last about
three weeks and can work for none of them, which is why an ostiary discourages the practice
without ever saying so, and why a cell with more than one is close to losing the hands it
has.

## The First Graft

He took his title from the brood rather than from the church he borrowed the rest of the
vocabulary from, and every hand notices it. The ranks beneath him are liturgical because a
rite needs liturgy and most people need a rite. His own title is medical, and it names him
rather than an office.

He has held at stage 3 for decades and has no intention of moving. A graft deepens with
rest, so holding a stage is not passivity and cannot be achieved by doing nothing. It costs
him a program of magical care administered in the exact proportion required, and it
consumes most of what a cell brings him. He has never been Unmeasured. He takes his measure
every morning at a hatch six feet from where he sleeps, and he takes his restorations
besides.

The cult's official position is that the First Graft remains where he is so that there is
somebody left who can still explain the book. A faction of Ciboria hold that the man who
tells every hand to go all the way has arranged never to arrive. He does not argue with
them, and he has outlived every faction that has raised it.

He was a lector, so he can run a house, keep a file, and read a case of any of the three
broods at a glance. Parties who expect a raving heretic meet a tired administrator who asks
careful questions about their symptoms and is genuinely relieved when the answers are good.

<Creature name="The First Graft">
  **Running it.** He is a tired administrator rather than a raving heretic, and he asks about the
  party's symptoms before anything else; the questions are careful, correct, and not a tactic. He
  does not open a fight, and he will hand over the True File's first three parts to anyone who asks.
  He will not hand over part V, surrender the sacristan, or be talked out of the argument, and the
  last of those is what usually ends the conversation. When it ends he fights like a scholar — Wall
  of Force to shut a corridor, Greater Invisibility to be elsewhere, Counterspell held for the
  party's caster, and Instar not at all while there is any prospect of the conversation resuming.
</Creature>

## Working with the Necrophore

The cult does not venerate the necrophore. It has an arrangement with one, which the
Lazaret considers the worse of the two.

The problem the arrangement solves is waste. A hand who reaches stage 4 becomes something,
and the cult counts that as the point of everything it does. A hand who dies short of it
becomes nothing at all, and by the book's own argument has been emptied for no purpose.

Sallow cases are not the problem, whatever a cell's newer hands assume. A sallow assignment
runs its course, the host dies of it, and the [Quagdam](/brood-and-bloom/chapter-4/#c-quagdam)
works its way out over the following days and leaves for standing water. The fluke fed the
whole way and goes on feeding after the death, so a sallow corpse is spent by the time
anything else could be laid in it. The cult counts this the most complete Translation of
the three, which is why a Kenotics that comes up sallow is met with congratulation rather
than grief.

The second offer covers the failures instead. Hands killed on the road or in a house. Hands
whose Kenotics did not take, where the parasite fed and nothing rooted. Hands who expelled
a graft, deliberately or by accident, and are now as empty as the day they arrived. Hands
who changed their minds and were brought back.

None of them are executed, and the cult would reject the word. They are given the second
offer, which is done at the ground, within a day, by a sexton, without ceremony of any
kind. The True File is explicit that the manner does not matter and freshness does. That is
the whole of the doctrine on the subject, and it is the passage the Lazaret quotes when it
is asked why the order treats a cell as a colony.

A necrophore lays in the dead and wants nothing from the living, which makes it the only
brood the cult can deal with as a supplier rather than as a subject. A body laid in still
produces. The book calls what comes out the second offer, distinguishes it carefully from
Translation, and does not claim the two are equal.

A cell of any age therefore keeps ground as well as a clutch: a barrow, a plague pit, a
field nobody works any more. Bodies go there fresh and they go there quickly, because a
body is useful for a day and worthless after. Cells that have held the same ground for
years are dangerous out of all proportion to the number of hands living on them.

## Refusing the Sporophore

The First Graft rejects the sporophore, and the rejection is doctrine rather than distaste.

A sporophore does not want a person. It wants the ground the person is standing on, and it
takes a wall or a field or a wood with the same appetite it takes a body. The True File
argues that a brood offering nothing to any individual has nothing to offer at all, and
that a hand who kneels to one has mistaken scale for generosity.

Hands who disagree exist anyway. Cells near contaminated ground produce them reliably, and
the cult has no procedure for the problem, because the book never anticipated a heresy
inside a heresy. What becomes of those hands varies by cell and is never written down.

## The order's position

The Lazaret does not recognize the Kenotics as a rite. Its files record voluntary
implantation, the date, the line, and nothing else. No house has ever refused to admit a
hand, because a house does not turn out a patient, and no house has ever cured one, because
the patient does not want it and a Lazaret officer is taught no restoration magic to press
on them anyway.

The book itself is the harder problem. The order publishes what it learns on the principle
that a file nobody has read protects nobody, and the founding argument of the cult was
published under that principle by one of its own. The counter-work is kept at the first
house and copied nowhere. Lectors disagree in writing about whether that is prudence or the
first crack in the only thing the order has ever been certain of, and the disagreement is
itself on file.
